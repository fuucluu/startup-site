Handmade pricing guide

Price the whole product, not just the materials.

A price can cover beads, wax, yarn, clay, or wood and still fail to pay for the work. A durable handmade price starts with the full unit cost, then accounts for selling fees and the profit needed to keep the business operating.

Build the true batch cost

Calculate one production batch before dividing down to a unit. Include direct materials, consumables, packaging, labor, expected waste, and a reasonable share of overhead.

True batch cost = materials + consumables + packaging + labor + waste + allocated overhead

True unit cost = true batch cost / sellable units produced

Use sellable units, not attempted units. If a batch produces 24 pieces but two normally fail quality control, divide by 22.

Pay for labor explicitly

Track the hands-on time for the batch and multiply it by an hourly labor rate. Do not hide labor inside an arbitrary markup. Separating labor makes it easier to see whether a process change actually improves the business.

Do not ignore overhead

Overhead is real even when it does not appear in the finished product. Examples include tools, software, studio space, electricity, website costs, labels, market research, and equipment maintenance. Choose a consistent allocation method and document it.

Model selling fees before choosing the price

Card and marketplace fees are usually calculated from the selling price, which means a price that only adds a fixed markup can miss the target. Model the actual percentage and fixed fee for the channel you use. Craft-fair pricing may also need to support booth costs that online pricing does not.

Separate margin from markup

Markup measures profit relative to cost. Margin measures profit relative to price. They are not interchangeable.

Markup = (price - cost) / cost

Margin = (price - cost) / price

A product that costs $10 and sells for $20 has a 100% markup but a 50% gross margin before selling fees.

Test the price against an actual event

A product price does not exist in isolation. Combine the average sale, product cost, booth expenses, payment fees, sales forecast, and total hours. Then calculate break-even transactions and expected hourly earnings.

Review actual results

After the event, record real sales, fees, product costs, event expenses, leftover inventory, and total time. Compare the result with the forecast. The gap is valuable: it tells you whether the next change belongs in pricing, product mix, event selection, or execution.
